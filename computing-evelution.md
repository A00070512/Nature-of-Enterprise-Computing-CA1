### 2.1 mechanical and early programmable foundations:

the history of modern computing go back to the 19th century with brithish mathematician charles babbage. in 1822 babbage designed the differance engione,
a mathematical calculator built to automate polynomial calculations.
building on this concept in 1837, he proposed the analytical engine, widly recognised as the first design for a general purpose,programmable computer featuring
an arithmetic logic unit, control flow and memory.
although never completed in his lifetime, ada lovelace recognized its potential and wrote the first algorithm for the machine execution.
nearly a century later, in 1941 german engineer konrad zuse built the Z3, the worlds first working electromechanical, fully automatic programmable digital computer,
proving that binary floating point arithmetic could be executed reliably by a machine.

### 2.2 first generation vacuum tube systems:

world war 2 speadup computing innovation, leading in the first generation of electronic digital computers powered by vacuum tubes.
in 1943 british engineers developed colossus to break encrypted wartime communications, while in the usa, john mauchly and j. presper eckert completed ENIAC (electronic numerical integrator and computer) in 1945.
capable of performing thousands of calculations per second, ENIAC occupied an entire room and required manual rewiring for different tasks. By 1951, the UNIVAC I became the first commercially produced digital computer in the usa, demonstrating that electronic computing could serve government agencies, census bureaus, and large enterprises rather than purely military research.

### 2.3 second generation translator and third generation integrated circuit systems:

During the 1950s, transistors were developed as an alternative to large, unreliable and power hungry vacuum tubes. Developed at Bell Labs, these small solid‑state components enabled efficient switching and completed the same functions as vacuum tubes but in a much smaller and more economical format (Computer History Museum, n.d.). Computers, such as the IBM 1401, therefore became smaller, quicker, more dependable and more economical to service, meaning that more companies, banks and universities began to implement mainframe computers (IBM, n.d.).

through the 1960s, the creation of the circuit (IC) signalled the third generation of computers. engineers were now able to embed transistors, resistors and capacitors onto one silicon chip, which vastly improved computational speed and further decreased production expenses (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.). Moreover, computer systems such as the IBM System/360 pioneered a range of computer sizes which utilised computer software. this solidified business computer usage within institutions and also established the basis for todays computer software environment (IBM, n.d.).
